# 2.1 Defining Your Market

## Two Questions to Help You Define Your Market

To begin the process of developing your market, you must first "define" it. To define your market, you will need to answer two basic questions,

1. *Who* buys the types of products or services you sell?
2. *Where* are they located?

In answering these questions, you will need to establish your market's physical boundaries. If, for example, you own a business specializing in the repair and installation of slate roofs, you can quickly discern that your most likely prospects are individuals, businesses and organizations who:

- Either currently own a slate roof or are interested in installing a new slate roof.
- Are in the geographic region that is a serviceable distance from your business.

Once this defining information is placed in your mind, you will have established the guidelines inside which the focused development of your market can begin to occur.

## Distribution Channels and Channel Groups

Depending upon how your products are brought to market, you may be using many different distribution channels to sell your products. Some distribution channels are direct and allow products to be sold straight from the manufacturer to the end-user. Others are indirect and require products be sold through a network of sales facilitators such as agents, brokers, distributors, reps, etc.

Whether you sell directly to end-users or through other agents - or both, you will have to identify with whom you need to regularly communicate to promote and develop sales activity. If your business uses more than one distribution channel, it may be useful to create a list of "channel groups" to separate the buyers from the distributors. Depending upon the makeup of these channel groups, you may wish to build a separate Prospect Factory tailored specifically for each group.

## High-Volume Markets and Specialty Markets

Some markets are high-volume in that there may be many prospects all located in a relatively small geographic area. In other instances, a market may require looking beyond state or even international boundaries to find an adequate supply of potential buyers.

Set up the parameters of your market so it is manageable in size. One way to adjust high-volume markets into more manageable size is to cut them down by territory. A question you might ask is,

> "Which prospects look like potential buyers in the ten (or, 15, 20, 25 etc.) Zip codes closest to the location of my business?"

Some examples of businesses that may benefit by reducing their markets by territory are: individual life insurance; automobile insurance; real estate; securities or investment sales; carpet cleaning services; lawn care services; window cleaning; blacktop resurfacing; radon or mold abatement; plumbing; house cleaning; house painting; pest control; tire service; etc.

Other markets are more specialized in that they focus on specific niche-types of prospects. Some examples of specialty markets are: consulting in international patent law; sale of customized lift systems or packaging machinery; sale of manufacturing process controls; sale of specialty machine tool services; specialty die cutting services; sale of industry specific advertising; sale of industry specific software; etc.

In specialty markets, territories may become larger and considerations about geographic location may be less of a concern for effective marketing. However, you will still need to know who your prospects are and where they are located no matter how far away they may be. In specialty markets, initial list development is an extremely important factor in identifying and locating an appropriate body of potential prospects. The good news though, is that specialty buyers are often more easily identifiable.

## Establishing a Minimum Qualification Standard

A "minimum qualification standard" is simply the definition or description of the minimum quality prospect with which you are willing to work and yet still derive a reasonable profit.

Some sales training programs emphasize formulating an idea of what your ideal prospect looks like before beginning prospecting. I do not dispute that understanding the characteristics of your ideal prospects can be useful. But, once you are past the richest or the best prospects in your market, you want to know who comes next.

It is beneficial to figure out who is the minimum quality prospect with which you are willing to do business for a variety of reasons. For example, you would not want to engage in business that causes you to lose money. Also, how high or low you set this standard can have a dramatic impact on your overall market size.

Generally, the more upscale your prospects are, the fewer of them there will be. The more average your prospects are, the more of them there will be. Set the standard so you can make a profit, but at the same time give yourself a manageable market size.

## Flexibility in the Size and Scope of Your Market

Flexibility is important as you define your market because it is sometimes difficult to predict an effective market size in advance. If you market in too wide an area, you may undermine the ability to deliver your goods or services. However, if you market in too small an area, the area you are working in may not contain enough qualified prospects on which to call.

Therefore, you must come up with a flexible idea of,

- A manageable number of prospects with which to work.
- A manageable territory within which to work.

At one point early in my career, I focused my marketing efforts on a county near the location of my office. At the time, I thought there were more than enough prospects in that county to keep me busy. I also thought that by working in a limited geographic area I would have an easier job of following up with and running appointments to see prospects. I further hoped I would begin to create built-in references for myself as I sold one after another of the businesses located in the same geographic area.

It turned out I was able to work through the initial prospect list I created for the targeted territory much more quickly than I first anticipated. Though this may have seemed like bad news at first; it was not. It simply meant I had a greater capacity to contact an even wider market. So, I made the decision to change my market's geographic parameters and push my business into more counties. In doing so, I was able to strike the balance I needed.

## Key Terms to Remember

**[[Channel Groups]]** – Different distribution channels, such as direct buyers versus resellers. Different channel groups may require that a separate Prospect Factory be created for each group.

**[[High-Volume Markets]]** – Markets with many prospects all located in a relatively small geographic area.

**[[Specialty Markets]]** – Markets that focus on specific niche-types of prospects. Territories may become larger and considerations about geographic location may be less of a concern for effective marketing.

**[[Minimum Qualification Standard]]** – The definition or description of the minimum quality prospect you are willing to work with and yet still make a reasonable profit.
