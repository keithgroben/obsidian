# 2.7 Mastering One-Pagers

## Defining a One-Pager

As mentioned earlier, a One-Pager is a one-page letter or form that carries a specific message about a particular product or service you sell along with a brief, general description of your business.

A One-Pager is not intended to sell a product or service directly. Instead, it is intended to assess a prospect's current level of interest for a product or service. It always includes a reply section which prompts the prospect to complete and return the form if he or she would like more information.

For example,

> Complete this form and return it by FAX,
> mail or email for more information!

One-Pagers include your complete contact information including name, company name, address, phone and fax number, and e-mail address.

Typically, a One-Pager is sent by itself for one letter. However, if you supplement this system with referred leads, you may wish to include a cover letter for the purpose of identifying the nominator.

## Sticky/Adhesive Notes

Frequently, I attach adhesive notes to One-Pagers along with a brief handwritten message. This practice has been proven through research to be particularly persuasive. Typically, I use this technique only with One-Pagers I am sending by first-class mail and as part of follow-up after a day of calls.

On the adhesive note, I write something brief, such as,

> "Fred, Thought you might find this helpful. Thanks, Ted"

> "Jane, Complete and return for quotes! Best, Ted"

> "Alice, Let me know if you need any help! Thanks, Ted"

Adding this note personalizes what I send. Some argue doing so is even more effective than attaching a formal cover letter (I tend to agree). I favor this practice because it is quick, easy to execute, and falls in line with my overall goal of building a personal connection with prospects.

## When to Use One-Pagers

You can also send One-Pagers in a variety of circumstances. Some examples are,

- One-Pagers are sent to prospects who would like more information about your business.
- One-Pagers are used to open the door to opportunities beyond the scope of an initial contact (as explained earlier in our discussion about Opportunity Codes).
- One-Pagers help communicate with prospects who are difficult to reach by phone.
- One-Pagers help communicate with prospects on our "Do Not Call" lists.
- One-Pagers help communicate with prospects who express future or marginal interest.
- One-Pagers are used in recurring direct mailings.

Though One-Pagers are sent under many different circumstances, they all come back the same way, as Finished Leads. Every time I receive a completed One-Pager, I have found what I am looking for, a currently interested prospect, and I can move my business forward.

## Must Haves in a One-Pager

One-Pagers should always have:

- A flag line introducing or leading into a small paragraph (or, two) relating to one or more of the services you provide.
- A brief, description of your business that includes your unique value proposition or "UVP."

## Writing a Flag Line

A "flag line" is the headline at the top of the page on a One-Pager. It is printed in bigger type or boldfaced, and its text is typically centered on the page. It may make a statement or ask a question. It is used to predict a product, service, or a potential benefit of something you sell.

Questions are my favorite form of flag lines.

Here are some examples:

> How do you know you aren't
> paying too much for your widgets?

> Why not consider looking into
> remanufactured widgets?

> How often are you shutting down your line to recalibrate your widgets?

> How many more widgets would you sell if
> you could systematically generate
> large numbers of qualified sales leads?

Flag lines do not have to be questions, but they must be provocative and lead the prospect to read further.

Examine your business and look in the areas of:

- Product features
- Cost savings
- Service
- Selection

How do your products and services help customers get what they want in these areas? What kinds of problems does your company, product or service solve? The answers to these questions should enable you to come up with at least a few good flag lines.

### Online Resources for Flag Lines

Take a moment to search how to write eye-catching titles or headlines online. Most of the results you will see relate to writing headlines for sales letters, blogs or articles. Headline writing has been studied and tested extensively by marketing experts. It is worthwhile to spend some time developing your skills in this area.

For example, some suggest that a headline question should not begin with a verb, because such headlines lead too quickly to dismissive "Yes" or "No" answers. Purveyors of this line of thought favor asking questions that begin with "what" or "how" believing such questions spur deeper and more persuasive introspection on the part of the reader.

## Writing the Supporting Paragraphs

After the flag line, write a short paragraph or two that follows up on and supports the flag line. Remember, the intent here is only to pursue current interest and not to make a concrete offer to "sell" anything.

Some copywriting experts prefer first highlighting a challenge the reader may face and then pivoting to solutions that may be of help. Because there isn't much space available, you'll have to keep things tight. The goal is to lead prospects to "learn more" by replying to you.

> *Note: Depending on your industry, any One-Pager you use should include necessary verifications and disclaimers and meet all requirements for supervisory review as required by law.*

## Varying the Flag Line and Its Supporting Paragraphs

The flag line and its supporting paragraph(s) are typically the only things that change from one One-Pager to the next. Because you will be sending One-Pagers to your prospects again and again over time, expect to vary the flag line and its supporting paragraphs to keep what you send fresh.

One idea is to format the flag line and its supporting paragraphs as a kind of mini newsletter. Most newsletters are not very proactive because they characteristically do not prompt prospects for current interest. One-Pagers, however, are highly proactive because, by design, they always prompt a prospect to reply if he or she has current interest in learning more about your products or services.

The fact that there is only a limited amount of physical space on the page for the flag line and its support is both good and bad. It is bad because you do not get to say much, but it is good because whatever you say will be brief.

If, after you have included all the necessary elements in your One-Pager, you still see extra space available, come back and develop the flag line and its support section in more detail.

## Writing a Brief, General Description of Your Business

Everything from this point on will stay about the same in every One-Pager you write. The flag line and its support paragraphs pinpoint the helpful nature of a product, product feature or service, but here we flip that over. The goal is to provide a more general description of your business and how you help customers.

As you compose this section, keep in mind that the client is the hero and you are merely the guide. Customers care first about solving their problems so keep the focus on helping them do so.

Examples of a brief, general description of a business read something like,

> "At XYZ Engineering, LLC we serve clients in the areas of design, engineering, construction, maintenance and regulatory compliance for Class I, II and III dams. For your peace of mind, we are committed to the highest possible industry standards."

Or,

> "XYZ Painting, LLC assists commercial and residential customers with painting needs. You deserve the most beautiful and longest lasting finished project. To help you get there, we are bonded and insured, and use paint from only the highest quality manufacturers."

The challenge is to briefly describe who you are, what you do, and how you help the customer get what he or she wants. This is equivalent to the "elevator speech" for your company. If you only had 15 seconds to describe who you are and what you do for people, what would you say?

## Start with "Why?"

It has become increasingly popular for marketing messages based less on "what" a company does or makes, but, rather, "why" the company does business in the first place.

An excellent TEDx video available online from author Simon Sinek details the essentials of the "Why?" based marketing approach. Check it out and see if you can use the power of "Why?" to help you describe your business.

## Unique Value Proposition

Another useful concept to apply as you craft a general description of your business is to define a Unique Value Proposition (UVP) or Unique Selling Proposition (USP).

A UVP or USP is used to define what differentiates you from your competition.

- What unique problems do your products and services solve for customers?
- Does your company offer a disruption or break from the status quo relative to your competition?
- Are you personal, local, or nationwide?
- Is the way you help customers distinctly innovative or cutting edge?

Constructing a UVP is not to be confused with puffery or boasting about how great you are. It is to describe in a concrete way how you bring value to your customers and differentiate yourself in the marketplace.

## Adding a Call to Action

After the general description comes the call to action. It is short and to the point. It provides specific instructions about what the prospect should do next if he or she has an interest.

It can be as simple as saying,

> **To learn more, complete and return**
> **this form by fax, mail, or e-mail!**

I usually center and bold this statement and use a larger font size, so it stands out. The key is to make things easy by letting an interested prospect know exactly what to do.

## Designing a Reply Section

The One-Pager's reply section is extremely important. It must always be easy for a prospect to complete and return the reply section of a One-Pager. However, with any reply section, you face a choice. How much detail should you ask for in a reply?

- In general, the more you ask for in a reply, the fewer but higher-quality replies you will receive.

Conversely, the less you ask for in a reply, the more replies you receive, but the quality of the respondents may be reduced.

The balance between volume and quality is one you will have to test over time. In my original use of One-Pagers, I began with a relatively extensive reply section. I asked for enough information to get back to the prospect with a detailed quote. This compressed my follow-up time and worked well in my business, but conditions may be different for yours.

In some businesses, you may be able to collect more detailed information in subsequent contacts. In the world of opt-in, email marketing, some marketers find it enough to collect only an email address first and then tease out more information in subsequent messages.

As you decide how to proceed, you may want to emphasize convenience at first. Later, if you are getting more replies than you can handle, congratulations! Perhaps then you can tighten things up by requiring more from a prospect in reply or by raising your standards for a minimum prospect.

## Avoid Saying, "Call if You're Interested"

When I create a One-Pager I avoid using a call to action that says, "Call if you're interested." It is unwise to send out a letter instructing prospects to call if interested unless you are set up to properly handle such calls. If you have qualified and trained staff ready to accept such calls, then go ahead and say, "Call if you're interested!" If not, it is much better to have replies faxed, mailed, or emailed back to you.

Because initial interest is characteristically fleeting, I try not to cause a prospect to formulate an expectation of talking to someone who can help if I cannot accommodate that expectation. Instead, I prompt prospects to "complete and return" their replies. This enables me to set aside designated times to address responses on my own terms and not out of the blue.

Once a prospect has heard back from me and has a better idea of who I am, I have no problem saying, "Call if you have any further interest." or "Call with any questions!" but I avoid saying it initially.
