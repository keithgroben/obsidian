# Part 1: Groundwork

Prospecting using this system begins with advanced preparation or groundwork. This includes spending time thinking about the products or services you sell and determining your insurance status.

1. *Who buys your products and services?* (In some cases, the "buyer" may be replaced with a person or entity that facilitates the buying of your products or services. This could include sales representatives, brokers, agents, etc.)

2. *Where are these buyers located?* (i.e. What geographical location?)

Once you know what kind of person (or business) buys (or facilitates the buying of) the types of products or services you sell, and where they are located, you create a general description or "avatar" of your typical buyer (or, again, facilitator).

Here are two sample general descriptions:

1. "All single-box widget manufacturers located in the states of Washington, Missouri, Ohio, Florida and Maine transacting between $10 and $5 million in sales per year."

2. "Any business or organization operating within a 100-mile radius of XYZ radio station's transmitter(s) that transacts over $1 million per year in sales that could benefit from radio advertising."

The next part of groundwork is to take this buyer description and apply it to a variety of primary sources (i.e. published lists, online directories, etc.) for the purpose of identifying potential prospects. In doing this, your goal is to put together a well thought out list that includes the names, addresses and phone numbers of many, ideally thousands of, potential prospects.

It's notable from the very start, this system is not relationship based. The prospect list created is a top-down catalog of your market. There is no prerequisite that you know or "know someone who knows" any prospect prior to his or her name being added to your list. Instead, you attempt to gather a broad array of prospects whose most significant common link is only how well you believe they match your buyer description.

Groundwork also involves setting in motion an ongoing effort to collect primary research about the prospects on your list. This research is "primary" because you do the research yourself.

Primary research can be collected through a combination of:

- Studying media and data sources (i.e. online resources, directories, trade publications, etc.).
- Conducting information surveys.
- Using personal observation.

The information you collect through research will be used over time to upgrade or downgrade the prospects on your list. Prospects are upgraded or downgraded based on their relative likelihood of becoming buyers of your goods or services.
