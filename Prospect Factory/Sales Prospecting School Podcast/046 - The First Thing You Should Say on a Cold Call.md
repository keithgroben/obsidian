# 046 - The First Thing You Should Say on a Cold Call
## Summary
John Stephenson and Ted Stevenot debate the very first words to say on a cold call, framing it as a choice between two delivery models: "unloading" the full pitch at once (Jeb Blount's Fanatical Prospecting approach) versus Ted's "chunking" method of bite-sized pieces the prospect can actually process. Treating the listener like an information-processing algorithm with limited working memory (about four items), Ted walks through his opening array: name, company, what you are, then a pause, then asking to speak with the buyer. He argues pausing lets prospects self-qualify, and that chunk-by-chunk delivery yields a real, interactive conversation rather than overwhelming noise.
## Key Takeaways
- Open with just three pieces of information (your name, your company, and what you are) then pause, so the prospect can register who you are and self-select out if they truly aren't a fit, which is itself useful [[3.1 Call Objectives]].
- Deliver your pitch in digestible chunks rather than unloading a full paragraph, because the human brain can only hold about four packets of information at once and excess detail just becomes noise the prospect discards [[3.3 General Overviews on Calling]].
- Treat the prospect as an information-processing algorithm: whatever you say gets filtered through 'does this matter to me,' so a sloppy opener that 'computes' to 'sales pitch' or 'manipulation' collapses the call before your message lands [[Professional Speech]].
- Ask to 'speak with the person who handles widgets,' because that phrasing opens a fallback ladder (transfer, voicemail, send mail) and turns a no-contact into a [[Plus List]] add or a qualified removal [[3.2 The Five Basic Outcomes to Most Calls]].
- Favor what, how, and when questions over yes/no questions, since open questions trigger more thought and sidestep the reflexive 'no' people give to protect a boundary [[Call Objective]].
- Use a 'because' statement to give a simple reason for your ask; the Cialdini copier-line experiment shows a reason alone, even a trivial one, dramatically raises compliance.
- Remember the call replaces a Google search or ad click as the proactive on-ramp into your follow-up machine, handing a qualified prospect into the lead-magnet and drip sequence [[5.2 Automation and Follow Up]].
- Think of cold calling like holding cards in euchre or rummy rather than dealing one at a time: you play the right response from a limited array based on what the prospect says, a pattern-matching skill built over thousands of calls [[6.4 Persistence]].
