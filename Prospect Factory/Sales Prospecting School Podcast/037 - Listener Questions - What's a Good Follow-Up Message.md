# 037 - Listener Questions - What's a Good Follow-Up Message
## Summary
A listener-question episode answering Brian's questions about follow-up. John and Ted argue that a good follow-up message is personalized, automated or fast, relationship-building, and always leaves an open door to act without hammering the prospect to buy. They split follow-up into two lanes: performance follow-up (responding to someone who raised their hand for a guide, quote, or lead magnet) and proximity follow-up (low-frequency, value-giving touches that keep the relationship warm), plus a third timely "special message" type. They cover cadence by complexity and origination, transitioning prospects from short drips into long-term lists, and how to close, build order steps, and use honest scarcity.
## Key Takeaways
- A strong follow-up message is personalized, automated or quick, and builds a relationship rather than just pushing a sale, mirroring the cadence principles in [[5.2 Automation and Follow Up]].
- John frames every message as laying a bridge across the table to the prospect, while always including a final action option so people can act immediately if they are ready, which connects to [[Section 5: Following Up with Finished Leads]].
- Ted separates follow-up into a performance lane (the prospect raised their hand and you must deliver) and a proximity lane (low-frequency value touches to maintain the connection), both of which sit inside [[5.3 Follow-Up Letters]].
- Performance follow-ups should fire fast, within hours to a couple of days, because the prospect is interested now, whereas proximity follow-ups can space out to weekly, monthly, or seasonal as covered in [[5.4 Sample Follow-Up Letters]].
- A good follow-up sequence tells your full story and surfaces services the customer may not know you offer, like a painter mentioning gutters, without the relentless hammering that erodes trust.
- Closing has two meanings here: a graceful wrap-up message that ends a follow-up series, and asking for the sale with clear order steps and honest scarcity rather than faux urgency, which ties to [[3.1 Call Objectives]].
- Use real, legitimate scarcity (limited charter slots, seasonal runs, renewal deadlines) instead of fake countdowns, and resist competing only on being the cheapest.
- Writing down what you say at every step builds an FAQ-style system over time, turning ad-hoc answers into a sustainable, automated follow-up machine that aligns with [[6.3 Learning Your Ratios]].
