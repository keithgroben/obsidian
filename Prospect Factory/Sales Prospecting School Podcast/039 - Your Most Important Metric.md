# 039 - Your Most Important Metric
## Summary
John and Ted argue that every business has one key metric that matters more than the rest, and most owners either never identify it or set wildly unrealistic expectations around it. Using client stories (a newsletter creator chasing fame, a startup, an email sender misreading open rates), they show how preconceived numbers trigger surprise, panic, and blame when reality lands. The fix is a six-step loop: pick a monetizable, controllable metric; measure current speed over a set time; track and measure; take improvement actions with managed expectations; then lather, rinse, repeat through testing and iteration.
## Key Takeaways
- Pick one metric you can actually control and that has a clear path to revenue; the hosts favor an email or return-path capture because it sets a [[Minimum Qualification Standard]] for a value exchange, while a number-one search ranking is poor because you can't directly control it.
- Activity-based metrics like number of calls, appointments scheduled, or emails captured are stronger leading indicators than the cash-register ring itself, which echoes the discipline behind [[6.3 Learning Your Ratios]] and the daily-volume mindset of [[4.1 Every Day of Phoning Is a Good Day]].
- A metric is meaningless without context: the client upset about a 37% email open rate was actually crushing a 12% industry average, so study external benchmarks before deciding whether your number is good or bad.
- Unrealistic expectations cause the surprise-panic-blame spiral (a Larry Wilson / Apollo-mission idea), so manage expectations up front to keep failed tests from feeling like personal failure, which ties into [[6.1 Dealing with Sales Rejection]].
- Establish your current speed first: count how many emails, walk-ins, or contacts you get in a normal week before trying to improve, the same way you'd assess a runner's baseline mile time.
- Make every result trackable and attributable, using coupon codes, samples, or source questions, so you know which action drove a sales bump rather than guessing across a blanket campaign.
- Treat testing as science, not emotion: A/B test headlines, colors, and offers, accept feedback from real customers, and run the loop repeatedly like an algorithm course-correcting toward the goal.
- Factor lifetime value into what a metric is worth, like the Polaroid film, Xbox games, or a health-equipment company that breaks even on the first machine and profits on consumables and follow-up sales over an eight-month buying cycle.
