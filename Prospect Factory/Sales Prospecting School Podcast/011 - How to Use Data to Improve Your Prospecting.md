# 011 - How to Use Data to Improve Your Prospecting
## Summary
John Stephenson and Ted Stevenot explain how to use data to sharpen prospecting, both old-school and digital. They define data as qualifying information that gets you closer to buyers and increases efficiency. Ted recounts capturing contacts in a Windows 95-era database, while John frames data as a feedback loop. They split data into direct (collected yourself) and third-party (leased or purchased), stress storing it somewhere accessible, sortable, stable, and automated, and contrast Google AdWords (search intent) with Facebook (interest and demographic targeting). A deep dive covers keyword research, negative keywords, orthogonal targeting, and protecting your budget through cheap testing.
## Key Takeaways
- Data is qualifying information that gets you closer to buyers and increases efficiency, so define your buyer with a [[Minimum Qualification Standard]] rather than chasing an unrealistic perfect prospect.
- Collect information that falls into your hands during high-volume outreach instead of pushing for it, then store it in a [[Customer Relationship Management Database (CRM)]] so nothing slips through the cracks.
- Data should be accessible, sortable, stable, and automated; the first three serve your understanding of the customer while automation drives your [[Follow-Up]] drip series and preserves your sanity at scale.
- Direct data is what you gather yourself through [[Cold Calling]], analytics, or social outreach, while third-party data is leased or purchased from list vendors and platforms like Facebook.
- When [[Defining Your Target Market]], build your avatar but use orthogonal signals (like credit scores predicting insurance risk) to reach the right buyers cheaply.
- Google AdWords captures people with active search or buying intent, so it is expensive or ineffective for obscure products that nobody knows to search for.
- Facebook excels at brand awareness and interest-based targeting, letting you back into your avatar by overlaying demographics, location, and interests.
- Protect yourself from the black hole of optimization by testing small (around $50 buys meaningful feedback), shipping something imperfect, and using negative keywords to avoid paying for irrelevant clicks.
