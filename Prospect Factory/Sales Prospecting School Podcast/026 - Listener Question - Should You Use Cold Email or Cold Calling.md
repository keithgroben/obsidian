# 026 - Listener Question - Should You Use Cold Email or Cold Calling
## Summary
John and Ted answer a listener (David) who is launching his first outbound campaign and asks whether to use cold email or cold calling, and whether to blast every contact at a company or target only the decision maker. They distinguish cold email from permission email, judge outreach against three tests (sustainable, survivable, effective), and dissect a heavily personalized cold email that took hours per recipient. Their verdict: cold email rarely survives spam filters or pays off, so start with a phone call to find who handles the buy, ask the best way to reach them, qualify for interest, then nurture with permission email.
## Key Takeaways
- Judge every outreach method against three tests: sustainability (can you keep doing it), survivability (does the rejection and energy cost let you continue), and effectiveness (does your message reach the goal), and weigh cold email and cold calling against all three before choosing.
- Cold email means no prior contact, while permission email follows someone raising their hand; the hosts strongly prefer permission-based outreach because cold blasts trigger spam complaints that can get your sending account locked and poison a good list.
- The most effective cold email they reviewed took three to four hours per recipient to personalize, which fails the survivable and sustainable tests even when it works, since you could make hundreds of [[3.4 Sample Calls|calls]] in that same window.
- Calling first lets you ask who handles the purchase and the best way to reach them, which solves the decision-maker problem and respects the prospect's contact preference instead of forcing them into your channel.
- A 'no' on the phone usually arrives bundled with [[Part 4: Qualifying Information|qualifying information]] like 'we reassess in July' that you will almost never get from an unanswered email, turning rejection into useful market research.
- Permission email shines for the follow-up and nurture stage after a call, delivering small, helpful touches over time rather than one overloaded sales pitch, which maps to the synergy between calls and [[5.2 Automation and Follow Up|automation]].
- Use the phone call to qualify for interest and pain, not just budget and fit; ask about their story, challenges, and willingness to act before investing in expensive sends like a FedEx package or a coffee mug.
- Avoid blind pitching and over-sending: positioning yourself with a unique approach keeps you from being commoditized down to price, and you should spend gift money only on the few prospects you have already qualified, an idea tied to [[6.3 Learning Your Ratios|learning your ratios]].
