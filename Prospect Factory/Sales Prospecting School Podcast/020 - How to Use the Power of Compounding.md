# 020 - How to Use the Power of Compounding
## Summary
John Stevenson and Ted Stevenot apply Darren Hardy's "compound effect" to sales prospecting. Their core argument: judging a prospecting day only by short-term wins (a sale today) leads to despair and burnout, because objections feel like personal rejection. Instead, salespeople should consciously hunt for long-term value, the data, relationships, qualification, and skills that accumulate every day, even on days with zero sales. They explain how cold lists become warm personal lists over years, why disqualifying prospects is itself valuable, the role of CRMs and sortable data, the Pareto principle for spending only on the best 20%, and A/B testing as future-focused continuous improvement.
## Key Takeaways
- A prospecting day with no sales is not a wasted day; if you made calls and captured information you advanced your long-term value by disqualifying leads and identifying the better-quality prospects worth keeping on your [[Plus List]].
- Start from the strongest list you can get, a compiled [[Primary List]] in your wheelhouse, then call it like a survey and promote responders up the qualification ladder, the same sift-and-winnow logic behind [[2.2 Putting Together Your Prospect List]].
- Treat the call itself as a survey that can close comfortably on both sides yet still open all the way to an immediate sale, which connects directly to defining a clear [[Call Objective]] and the outcomes in [[3.2 The Five Basic Outcomes to Most Calls]].
- Capture key data in a sortable, codified form, not freeform notes; a [[Customer Relationship Management Database (CRM)]] (Zoho, Google Sheets, or even margin notes) only pays off later if the metrics can be sorted and queried.
- Apply the Pareto principle by spending money only on the self-identified top 20% of prospects who requested information, which is the engine behind [[2.8 Sending Mail to Your Plus List]] and the synergy described in [[4.2 Synergy Between the Plus List and Direct Mail]].
- Cold calling earns a bad reputation, but called consistently over years a cold list becomes a warm personal list of people who recognize your voice, which is why persistence pays, see [[6.4 Persistence]].
- Reframing each day around accumulating long-term value shields you from the emotional sting of short-term rejection, the same mindset shift covered in [[6.1 Dealing with Sales Rejection]].
- Use A/B testing as a champion-versus-challenger race across mail and other channels to find what produces strength over time, but expect message fatigue and keep multiple tests running, the continuous-improvement (kaizen) view tied to [[6.3 Learning Your Ratios]].
